What This Book Is About

This is not a story I made up. It's mine. Before I was a month old, doctors said I wouldn't survive. I did. I grew up with a mother who had no job but never once let me go hungry or miss school for lack of trying. I was sent home for school fees in Primary One, and that same afternoon, strangers walked into our home and changed my life. I lost my father's family's support along the way and found it from people who owed me nothing. In 2025, I walked out of a fatal accident without a single scratch. This book is the full account, chapter by chapter — not because my story is more special than yours, but because I need you to know that if I made it through all of that, still breathing, then so can you.

CHAPTER ONE

Two Weeks Old

I don't remember the room, obviously. I don't remember the doctor's face, or his voice, or the exact words he used. But I've heard the story enough times, from enough people who were actually there, that I can see it anyway — the way you can see a place you've never visited if someone describes it to you carefully enough, over and over, for years.

I was two weeks old. Whatever was wrong with me — and even now, the details of it are the kind of thing that gets summarized rather than explained, the way families do with the worst weeks of their lives — it was bad enough that a doctor sat my mother down and told her, plainly, that I wouldn't last a month. Not "there are risks." Not "we'll do what we can." A month. That was the number he gave her, like a return date on something she'd borrowed.

I think about that number sometimes. A month. It means that somewhere out there, on a calendar nobody kept, there was a day marked as the day I was supposed to stop existing. That day came and went decades ago now, and I was still here for it, and I've been here for every day since.

I don't know exactly what my mother did in those two weeks — what prayers she said, what she refused to accept, what it cost her to sit with a child she'd just brought into the world while being told not to get attached to the idea of keeping him. I know she didn't have money. I know she didn't have a job. I know that whatever fight she put up for my life, she put it up with nothing behind her except whatever it is that makes a mother refuse a doctor's timeline for her child.

This is the part of my story I understand the least and believe the most. I have no medical explanation for why I'm still here. I have no dramatic turning point to describe, no scene where a specialist rushed in and reversed the diagnosis. What I have is simpler and stranger than that: I was supposed to be gone in a month, and I wasn't, and nobody — not the doctors, not my mother, not me, all these years later — has ever fully explained why.

I've come to believe that's not a gap in the story. I think that's the actual point of it. Some things don't come with an explanation attached. They just come with an outcome, and you're left to decide what you believe about how you got there. I believe I was kept here. Not by luck, not by the hospital, not by anything I did, because I was two weeks old and had done nothing yet but exist. I believe I was kept here by God, on purpose, before I could talk, before I could understand a single thing about what was happening to me, before I had any say in the matter at all.

That's the part people skip when they hear stories like mine. They want to jump to the accomplishments — the scholarships, the surviving, the accident I walked away from without a scratch, all of it — like the interesting part is what I did with my life once I had one. But I think the first miracle is the one nobody gives me credit for, because I wasn't old enough yet to be given credit for anything. I didn't survive that month through willpower or grit or any of the things people like to praise in a story like this. I survived it because something bigger than a doctor's prognosis had already decided I wasn't finished.

I bring this up first, before anything else in this book, because I think it's the key to understanding everything that came after it. Every other chapter in this story — the school fees I couldn't pay, the family support that wasn't there, the accident that should have killed me in a van I had no business walking away from — all of it happened to someone who was never supposed to make it past one month old in the first place. Everything since then has been, in a sense, extra. Borrowed time that turned into an actual life.

If you're reading this and you're in a season where someone — a doctor, a circumstance, your own mind at 3am — has given you a timeline on how much longer something good can possibly last for you, I want you to sit with this chapter for a second before you move to the next one. I was given a timeline once too. It didn't hold. I'm not saying yours won't be hard, or that it won't cost you things you didn't want to lose. I'm saying the timeline isn't the whole story. It never has been. Not for me, and I don't believe it is for you either.

If you're still breathing, your story isn't over. I didn't come up with that line for a slogan. I came up with it because it's the only honest way I've ever found to describe what happened to me before I was even old enough to know it was happening.

The rest of this story — school fees, strangers who became family, a teacher's phone call, and the accident that should have ended it all — is coming, chapter by chapter.
