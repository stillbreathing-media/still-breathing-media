What This Book Is About

Grace Namono has spent nineteen years asking permission — from her husband, her pastor, her mother, her own fear — before she lets herself want anything for herself. This is the story of the year she stopped. Not in one dramatic scene, but in a hundred small refusals, starting with a business idea she'd buried for a decade and ending with a version of herself nobody asked her to become, but that God had been waiting for her to become anyway.

CHAPTER ONE

The Question She Kept Asking

Grace Namono had asked her husband the same question four times in three years, and she was ashamed to realize, standing in the kitchen with the dish towel still in her hands, that she was about to ask it a fifth time.

"I want to talk to you about the tailoring shop again," she said, and even to her own ears her voice had the particular carefulness of someone approaching a sleeping dog. "The space near the market is still available. Nakato said the landlord would hold it another month if I paid a deposit."

Musa didn't look up from his phone right away, which was its own kind of answer. He was not a cruel man — Grace wanted to be fair about that, even now, even in the retelling of this to herself that she did almost nightly in her head. He had never once raised his voice at her about the shop. He simply had a way of receiving the idea that made it very clear, without ever saying so directly, that the idea was not a serious use of anyone's time.

"Grace." He said her name the way he always did when he was about to be reasonable at her. "We've talked about this. Where is the deposit money coming from? What happens to the children's dinner while you're at a shop all day? Who is going to buy dresses from a tailor in this economy?"

Every question was practical. That was the maddening thing about it, the thing that made it so hard to be angry in a way she could point to. He wasn't saying no because he didn't believe in her. He was saying no in the shape of concern, wrapped so carefully in her family's welfare that objecting to it felt like objecting to her own children's dinner.

"I've done the numbers," Grace said. "I showed you the numbers in March. I could start small — alterations first, then—"

"And who takes care of things here while you're figuring out your numbers?" Musa set his phone down now, which meant he considered the conversation serious enough to require his full attention, which somehow made it worse. "I'm not saying never, Grace. I'm saying now is not the time. When the children are older. When we've saved more. There will be a right time for this."

There will be a right time for this. Grace had heard some version of that sentence in nearly every year of her adult life, from nearly every person whose approval she'd learned, very young, to require before she let herself want something. Her mother had said it about Grace's dream of studying fashion design, decades ago, in a different kitchen, in nearly the same tone. Her pastor had said something close to it two years ago when she'd mentioned wanting to lead a women's group instead of just attending one — *let's pray about the timing, Grace, submission is also a kind of faith.* Even her own mind said it to her, some mornings, in a voice she'd absorbed so early she could no longer tell whose it had originally been.

She went back to the dishes. This was, she would understand much later, the actual pattern — not that she was told no, but that being told no ended the conversation, every time, for nineteen years, because some old and well-trained part of her believed that a no from the right person was simply the truth arriving, rather than one opinion in a decision that was supposed to be hers to make.

That night, after Musa was asleep, Grace sat at the small table in the kitchen with the lamp turned low, and took out the notebook where she kept the numbers he'd asked to see in March. She hadn't stopped working on them, these three years of being told it wasn't the time. She'd just stopped mentioning that she hadn't stopped.

She looked at the figures for a long time. The deposit. The sewing machines she already owned, sitting under a cloth in the spare room, bought secondhand two years ago with money she'd told Musa was for her sister's wedding contribution — a lie so small and so old now that she'd almost forgotten it was one. The three women from church who'd already said, quietly, that they'd buy from her the day she opened. The landlord's month-long hold on the space, which meant this decision, unlike every other one in her marriage, actually had a deadline attached to it whether Musa acknowledged that or not.

Grace closed the notebook. She did not decide anything that night, not in any way she could have named out loud. But she noticed something, sitting there in the low lamp light, that she had never quite let herself notice before: she was going to ask him a fifth time, because that was the pattern, and some part of her already suspected that this time, for reasons she couldn't yet explain to herself, she might not wait for his answer before she acted anyway.

She just didn't know yet what that would cost her, or who she'd have to become to survive the asking.

The rest of Grace's story — her mother's buried dream, the first no she ever gave back, and the day she finally became her — is coming, chapter by chapter.
